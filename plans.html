Paid plans in Satispay

Why launching plans?

Since its launch, most of Satispay's revenue has been tied to transactions, so it fluctuate with the seasons and with how much people spend each month. As the product grew, with cards, IBAN, savings and investments, the company needed a steadier, recurring revenue stream.

The strategic and rational answer was a subscription program, being able to avoid seasonality for the business and transform the product into a full financial companion, with a wide offer, able to compete also with other market players.

It started in Q3 2025 with Plus (3,99 €/month), a single plan built around everyday benefits: Instant top-ups, extra Points and reduced fees on yielded money boxes. In Q2 2026 it became a three-tier offer, adding Metal (12,99 €/month) and Velvet (39,99 €/month), each with its own Mastercard debit card, airport lounges, partner perks and much more.

As sole product designer of the Growth domain, I designed every experience in the plans: how users discover them, how they choose and subscribe, how they manage, switch and cancel, and how they use their perks, from airport lounges and Fast Track to partner subscriptions.

The challenges

Satispay built its name on being free and fast. Adding paid plans to an app like that meant solving problems a subscription-first product never has to face:

  • Paying for what used to be free: users had never paid for Satispay. Every plan had to show its value in the first seconds, or it would feel like a fee on something they already had.
  • Not touching the payment flow: payments are the reason people open the app. Upsells could never add friction to paying, so discovery had to live around it, not inside it.
  • Too much to explain: the top tier bundles up to 15 benefits across Points, investing, cards, insurance, travel and partner subscriptions. Three tiers multiplied the risk of overload.
  • Benefits that depend on the user: some perks, like instant top-ups, are only available to users with a certain account status, which can change after subscribing. The plan had to stay honest about what each user really gets.
  • Rules from day one: a 14-day right of withdrawal, pro-rata refunds, renewals paid from the Satispay balance, and card and lounge costs for people who leave early. All of it had to be clear, and compliant with EU consumer law.

User testing: which benefits matter

Before deciding what went into each tier, and in what order, we asked users. Over 700 people took a preference test: eight times in a row, they saw five benefit categories out of 11 and picked the most and the least important one. Repeating the choice forces a ranking, instead of the "everything sounds nice" answers a normal survey gets.

The results grouped into clear clusters:

  • Hero perks: video streaming (picked first 32% of the time) and cinema tickets (21%) together covered more than half of all choices, across every age group.
  • Second tier: news and AI tools. News mattered more to people who already pay for a subscription, AI to younger users.
  • Situational: food delivery and mobility climbed only for people who already use them often.
  • Bottom of the list: VPNs, even though competitors lead with them.

The biggest takeaway: most people care about three to five types of service. So instead of the longest possible list, each plan leads with its few strongest benefits, grouped by category, and the full list is one tap away. I combined this with a benchmark of Revolut, N26 and Monzo, plus subscription-led products like Duolingo and Preply, to keep the patterns familiar.

Managing the user lifecycle

With three paid tiers, users don't just subscribe or cancel: they move between plans. Each move follows different rules, so each one got its own flow.

Upgrade: fast, because the user is getting more. The switch is immediate and the new benefits unlock right away. The old plan is refunded, fully within the first 14 days or for the unused days after, and the new one is charged in full. We considered showing a refund preview, but chose fewer taps and show the refund in the activity list instead. For example, someone on Plus who upgrades to Metal five days later gets their €3.99 back and pays €12.99.

Downgrade: transparent, because the user is giving something up. After the first 14 days, the higher plan stays active until the end of the billing period, then switches automatically. Within 14 days, the switch is immediate with a full refund. Either way, the flow ends on a recap of the refund, the new plan's cost and the date it starts.

Unsubscription: honest, with a reason to stay. The flow starts with what you'll lose, then a short survey on why you're leaving, then a recap. It spells out the refund, the date benefits end, and any card or lounge costs if you leave within 10 months. Nothing is hidden behind customer support.

Renewal: no surprise cancellations. If a renewal fails for lack of balance, benefits stay active during a retry period instead of disappearing overnight.

How plans made a real impact

In about seven months, the program grew from around 2,000 to 25,000 subscribers across the three plans: around €900k in annual recurring revenue from a business line that didn't exist a year earlier. Three levers made the difference.

A paywall built to compare. Plus, Metal and Velvet sit in tabs on one screen, each led by its card and its best benefits. Billing comes in a second step. A first A/B test, savings-led versus feature-led, showed no real difference: the layout mattered more than the message. The two-step paywall then raised conversion by over 30%, and adding the new benefits pushed it further. Conversion at full price stayed above the 2% target for weeks and peaked at 3.2%.

Upsells at the right moment. Each upsell shows one benefit when users feel the cost of not having it: right after paying a top-up fee, on a Business Up payment, or on the Points page. A frequency cap keeps them from feeling pushy. In their first month, upsells brought in 1,900 subscribers. The Points page got the most clicks, the top-up moment converted best.

Retention by design. Getting people in was half the job. 30-day churn dropped from 10% to 6% as new benefits arrived, and a few targeted fixes kept it there:

  • Renewal fallback: many early cancellations came from users without enough balance on renewal day. Letting the renewal pull the missing amount from their bank account fixed it at the root.
  • Free trials over discounts: trial users churned less than those on a discounted first month, so trials became the main way in.
  • Plan identity: the plan colour on the avatar ring, the Profile badge and the welcome screen keeps the plan visible across the app, as a daily reminder of what you pay for.

Reflections

What went well

Plans became part of the product. They started as an add-on and now connect the card, savings, investments and payments. Close to 30% of new users choose a plan when they start using the app.

Being clear about the hard parts paid off. Refunds, withdrawal periods and card costs are where a subscription earns or loses trust. Spelling out the numbers in every flow, even when it added a step, was always worth it.

What we could have done earlier

  • Test willingness to pay, not just preference. The survey told us what people like, not what they'd pay for. Cinema ranked high, NordVPN low, yet after launch NordVPN was the most used perk (around 40%) while most others stayed under 10%. Proposal: test benefit bundles at real prices before locking the tiers.
  • Design the first week after subscribing. People stay for the few benefits they use, and many never found theirs. Proposal: a short onboarding that activates two or three perks matched to each user's habits, and paying partners on activation rather than eligibility.
  • Plan for renewal failures from day one. The bank fallback came after we saw the churn. Proposal: a reminder a few days before renewal and a low-balance check built into the first release.
  • Meet users earlier. Traffic from campaigns converted much better than organic visits to the paywall. Proposal: offer plan choice and the free trial during sign-up, when the user is already deciding how to use the app.
  • Size tests to our traffic. The first paywall test stopped before reaching significance. Proposal: test bolder changes, or run tests on campaign traffic, to get answers in weeks instead of months.
